Finding & Working Locations

The best street photographs emerge from understanding place. Learn to scout, evaluate, and work locations to maximize your photographic opportunities.

Scouting Locations

Great street photography rarely happens by accident. The masters knew their cities intimately—where light falls at certain hours, where crowds gather, where interesting characters appear. Scouting transforms random wandering into purposeful hunting.

Mental mapping of your city. Mark intersections, light patterns, and gathering spots. The scouting route connects potential locations.

What to Look For

Scout for strong backgrounds, interesting light patterns, natural frames, and places where people naturally congregate or pass through. Note how light changes through the day, and identify spots that only work at specific times.

  • Walk without your camera first — observe without the pressure to shoot
  • Note compass directions — understand where sun rises and sets
  • Return at different times — morning, lunch hour, evening rush
  • Map your mental hotspots — build a personal geography of opportunities

Types of Street Locations

Different locations offer different photographic opportunities. Understanding what each type provides helps you choose the right spot for the image you want to create.

Each location type offers distinct photographic opportunities. Match your intent to the location's character.

The Fishing Spot Technique

Instead of hunting through the streets, find a compelling background or scene—then wait. Like a fisherman who knows the good spots, you position yourself where interesting elements are likely to converge. The background does the heavy lifting; you just wait for the right subject to complete the frame.

The fishing spot technique: find a compelling stage, then wait for the right actor to enter your scene.

  • Pre-focus your camera — zone focus to eliminate delay
  • Be patient — good fishing spots can require 20+ minutes
  • Stay ready — the moment appears and vanishes in seconds
  • Work the spot — don't leave after one good shot

Time of Day Considerations

A location transforms throughout the day. The same street corner can offer entirely different photographs at dawn, midday, and dusk. Understanding these transformations lets you return to familiar spots with fresh purpose.

The same location offers six different photographs depending on when you arrive. Return repeatedly to unlock its full potential.

Key Insight

Most photographers only visit locations once. By returning at different times, you see what others miss. A boring midday location might transform into magic during blue hour. Know your spots at all hours.

Working a Single Location

There are two approaches to street photography: surface skimming many locations, or deep exploration of one. Both have merit, but mastering a single location often yields more distinctive work than brief visits to many.

Deep exploration of one location often reveals what surface skimming never finds. Familiarity breeds insight.

  • Exhaust the obvious — shoot the standard angles first to clear your mind
  • Change your height — crouch, climb, find elevated positions
  • Circle the location — photograph from every compass point
  • Stay through transitions — light changes, crowds shift, energy transforms

Tourist Spots vs Hidden Gems

Famous locations attract photographers like moths to flame. But the most authentic street photography often happens in overlooked neighborhoods. The question isn't whether to shoot tourist spots—it's how to see them fresh, or how to find alternatives that tell truer stories.

Authenticity exists on a spectrum. Tourist spots can yield genuine moments; hidden gems require discovery.

The 10-Block Rule

From any famous landmark, walk 10 blocks in any direction. The tourist density drops dramatically. The people you find are living their lives, not performing for cameras. This is where real street photography begins.

Indoor Street Photography

Street photography doesn't require streets. Indoor public spaces—malls, stations, covered markets, museums—offer controlled lighting, weather protection, and unique opportunities for human observation.

Indoor public spaces offer controlled environments for observation. Train stations, malls, and markets each have distinct characters.

Traveling for Street Photography

New cities offer what your hometown cannot: fresh eyes. Without preconceptions, everything becomes potential subject matter. The unfamiliar sharpens observation. But traveling also requires adaptation—different cultures, different rhythms, different approaches.

Travel photography follows predictable phases. Fresh eyes see everything; understanding takes time to develop.

  • Arrive early, stay late — dawn and dusk reward the dedicated
  • Walk before you shoot — scout first day, shoot second
  • Learn basic phrases — "may I photograph you?" opens doors
  • Embrace discomfort — unfamiliarity sharpens observation
  • Keep shooting at home — apply fresh eyes to familiar streets

Practice Exercise

Assignment: The One-Block Project

Choose a single city block. Visit it at dawn, midday, and dusk on three different days. Each visit, photograph for at least 30 minutes. Work every angle—the obvious shots first, then search for what only reveals itself to patient eyes. At the end, select your 10 strongest images. Notice how time transforms place.

Assignment: The Fishing Spot

Find a location with a strong background—dramatic light, geometric shapes, or compelling texture. Set your camera on a tripod or find a stable position. Pre-focus. Then wait. Stay for at least one hour. Let subjects enter your frame rather than chasing them. Review which images work and analyze why certain moments completed the composition.

Assignment: The 10-Block Walk

Start at a famous landmark in your city. Walk exactly 10 blocks in one direction. Then spend an hour photographing only what you find at your destination. Compare these images to photographs you might take at the landmark itself. Which tell more authentic stories?
