Equipment & Gear Philosophy

The tools of street photography are less important than the vision behind them. Learn to choose gear that serves your creative goals—not the other way around.

The Best Camera Myth

The most frequently asked question in street photography is "What camera should I buy?" The answer, while perhaps unsatisfying, is both simple and profound: the best camera is the one you have with you, the one you know intimately, and the one that becomes invisible in your hands.

Henri Cartier-Bresson's most iconic images were made with equipment far less capable than today's smartphones.

Key Insight

Gear Acquisition Syndrome (GAS) is the photographer's curse. Every hour spent researching cameras is an hour not spent making photographs. The masters of street photography—Cartier-Bresson, Winogrand, Klein—used equipment that would be considered primitive by today's standards. Their vision, not their gear, made the difference.

Camera Types Compared

Different camera types offer distinct advantages for street photography. Understanding their trade-offs helps you choose wisely—or embrace what you already own.

Each camera type excels in different areas. Notice how discretion and portability often inversely correlate with traditional "image quality."

  • DSLRs offer optical viewfinders and extensive lens ecosystems, but their size draws attention
  • Mirrorless cameras balance quality with compactness—the modern sweet spot
  • Premium compacts (Ricoh GR, Fuji X100 series) are beloved by street photographers
  • Smartphones are always with you—the camera that's present beats the one left at home

Focal Length Philosophy

The focal length you choose fundamentally shapes your relationship with your subjects and environment. Each classic street focal length offers a different way of seeing.

Wider lenses require you to get closer, creating more intimate—and more confrontational—photography. Each focal length encourages a different relationship with your subjects.

The Commitment Factor

A 28mm lens forces you to be within arm's reach of your subject. This requires confidence and commitment—you can't hide at a distance. Many street photographers find this constraint liberating: it eliminates hesitation and creates more powerful, immediate images.

One Camera, One Lens

The minimalist approach—one camera body, one fixed focal length—is not a limitation but a liberation. When you remove choice, you remove hesitation. The camera becomes an extension of your eye.

Constraints breed creativity. When you can't zoom, you must move. When you have no choice, you can only commit.

  • Learn to see in your focal length—eventually you'll pre-visualize frames without lifting the camera
  • Your feet become your zoom—physical engagement replaces optical distance
  • Eliminate the "what if"—accept what you have and work with it fully
  • Master one before adding—depth of knowledge beats breadth of options

The Inconspicuous Advantage

In street photography, being unnoticed often means capturing authentic moments. Smaller cameras change how people react to you—or rather, they don't react at all.

Camera size directly affects how subjects perceive and react to you. Smaller often means more authentic moments.

The Tourist Disguise

A small camera makes you look like everyone else. People unconsciously categorize you as "harmless tourist" rather than "professional photographer." This social camouflage grants access to moments that would otherwise close off the instant a large camera appears.

Essential Accessories

Most photography accessories are solutions looking for problems. Here's what actually matters in street photography—and what you can safely ignore.

✓ Essential

✗ Usually Unnecessary

  • A comfortable strap you'll wear all day matters more than any optical accessory
  • One extra battery is essential; five is paranoia
  • Good walking shoes are the most important piece of street photography gear you own
  • A small, unobtrusive bag that doesn't scream "expensive camera inside"

The Leica Mystique

No discussion of street photography gear is complete without addressing the elephant in the room: Leica. These cameras have achieved mythical status, but understanding why—and whether it matters—is essential.

Leica cameras are beautifully engineered tools, but alternatives costing 1/10th the price can produce equally compelling work.

The Real Question

Would owning a Leica make you a better photographer? Almost certainly not. Would it bring you joy? Possibly. The question isn't whether Leicas are good cameras (they are), but whether the investment serves your photography or merely your ego. Most legendary street photos were made with cameras far less expensive than today's Leicas.

Phone Street Photography

The smartphone has democratized street photography in unprecedented ways. The camera that's always with you, always connected, always ready. Modern phones are capable of serious work.

Modern smartphones combine discretion, capability, and ubiquity in ways no dedicated camera can match.

  • Pre-compose mentally—phone lag means the moment you see is not the moment you capture
  • Use burst mode liberally—storage is cheap, decisive moments are rare
  • Master low-light modes—computational photography excels here
  • Develop a consistent editing style—your phone's default processing is designed for social media, not art

Practice Exercises

Assignment: One Week, One Focal Length

Choose a single focal length (or tape your zoom lens to one position) and shoot exclusively with it for one week. Document how your seeing changes. By day seven, you should be pre-visualizing frames before raising the camera. Notice what you include and exclude, and how you move differently through space.

Assignment: Phone Only

Leave your "real" camera at home for a month. Shoot exclusively with your phone. Notice the freedom of having no bag, no decisions, no gear anxiety. Pay attention to moments you capture that you would have missed while your camera sat at home. At the end, evaluate: were the photos meaningfully worse?

Assignment: The Masters' Constraints

Research the equipment used by three street photography masters (Cartier-Bresson, Winogrand, Meyerowitz, etc.). Note how simple their setups were. Then photograph for a day using similar constraints: one camera, one lens, no chimping (reviewing on screen). Embrace the limitation as liberation.
