Post-Processing & Editing

The digital darkroom is an extension of your creative vision. Learn to enhance your images while preserving the authenticity of the moment you captured.

The Editing Philosophy

Post-processing in street photography walks a fine line. Unlike commercial or fine art photography, street photography carries an implicit promise of truth—that what we show existed in that moment. Our role as editors is to enhance what was there, not to fabricate what wasn't.

The editing spectrum: from untouched RAW to complete fabrication. Stay in the zone that enhances truth without inventing it.

The Guiding Principle

Ask yourself: "Could a witness to this scene verify my photograph?" If your edits change the answer from yes to no, you've crossed the line from enhancement to fabrication. Adjust exposure, fix white balance, crop—but don't add clouds, remove people, or composite multiple moments.

The Selection Process

Before any editing begins, you must cull ruthlessly. The selection process is where great photographers separate themselves from good ones. Your best work rises to the top only when you're willing to reject the rest.

The editing funnel: ruthless culling is what separates a portfolio from a hard drive full of snapshots.

  • First pass: Quick rejections only—remove obvious failures (blurry, mis-timed, badly exposed)
  • Second pass: Rate what's left—does this image have something to say?
  • Third pass: Compare similar shots—keep only the strongest from each situation
  • Time test: Wait a week, then review again with fresh eyes

The Order Matters

Work in this sequence: Exposure first (set overall brightness), then Highlights and Shadows (recover detail), then Whites and Blacks (set endpoints), and finally Contrast (for punch). This systematic approach prevents chasing your tail with conflicting adjustments.

Cropping Decisions

Cropping is the most powerful tool you have—and the most abused. Every crop should serve the image, either by removing distractions or strengthening the composition. Never crop just to "zoom in."

Cropping should remove distractions and strengthen composition. Maintain consistent aspect ratios in your portfolio.

  • Crop to strengthen: Does the crop improve the rule of thirds or leading lines?
  • Crop to simplify: Are you removing genuinely distracting elements?
  • Never crop to save: If the shot didn't work, accept it and move on
  • Maintain resolution: Aggressive crops reduce print quality—know your limits

Developing a Consistent Style

A recognizable editing style becomes part of your visual signature. This doesn't mean processing every image identically, but developing a coherent aesthetic language that ties your work together.

Style consistency ties individual images into a coherent body of work.

Building Your Preset Foundation

Create 2-3 base presets that represent your vision, then adjust individual images within that framework. Your presets shouldn't do everything—they should establish a starting point that you refine for each image's unique needs.

Color Grading Approaches

Color grading sets the emotional temperature of your work. Whether subtle or dramatic, your color choices communicate mood before the viewer consciously reads the content.

Six common color grading directions, each with distinct emotional associations.

Local Adjustments

While global adjustments affect the entire image, local adjustments let you paint light and attention exactly where you want them. This is the digital equivalent of dodging and burning in the darkroom.

Local adjustments direct the viewer's attention by controlling where the eye naturally travels.

Subtlety is Key

The best local adjustments are invisible. If a viewer can see your dodging and burning, you've gone too far. Build up adjustments gradually with low opacity passes. The goal is to guide attention, not to create obvious halos or gradients.

The "Straight Out of Camera" Debate

Some purists argue that true street photography should be untouched—"straight out of camera" (SOOC). This misunderstands both history and the nature of the medium. The question isn't whether to process, but how much serves the image.

The Historical Reality

Every master of street photography processed their work. Ansel Adams spent hours in the darkroom. Henri Cartier-Bresson worked with printers who understood his vision. Even your camera's JPEG is processed—just not by you. The choice isn't process or not process; it's who controls the interpretation.

  • When SOOC works: When conditions were perfect and camera interpretation matches your vision
  • When processing helps: Difficult lighting, bringing out shadow detail, correcting color casts
  • When processing hurts: When it becomes a crutch for poor capture or pushes beyond authenticity
  • The real question: Does this edit serve the photograph's truth or my ego?

Practice Exercises

Assignment: One Image, Five Interpretations

Take a single strong image and create five distinctly different edits: high-key bright, low-key dark, warm vintage, cool contemporary, and high-contrast black & white. Study how each interpretation changes the emotional impact. Which serves the content best?

Assignment: The Kill Session

Go through your last 500 photographs and delete everything that isn't genuinely strong. Be ruthless. If you hesitate, delete it. The goal is to end with no more than 25 images—your real portfolio, not your hard drive.

Assignment: Develop Your Preset

Create a single base preset that represents your visual style. Apply it to 20 different images from various lighting conditions. Refine until it provides a consistent starting point that requires only minor tweaks per image.
