Tutorials / Reflections, Windows & Mirrors

Through the Looking Glass

The city is full of doubled worlds. Every pane of glass, every puddle, every polished surface offers a second image laid over the first—an invitation to see more than what stands before you. Learning to read and compose with reflections is among the most sophisticated skills in street photography.

Why Reflections Matter

A reflection transforms a single moment into a layered one. Where a straightforward photograph shows one plane of reality, a reflection introduces a second—sometimes a third. The street is no longer just the street: it becomes a palimpsest of overlapping spaces, figures, and light. Reflections turn ordinary storefronts into stages, puddles into portals, and car windows into split-screen narratives.

The great street photographers have long understood this. Saul Leiter painted through rain-streaked glass. Vivian Maier found entire compositions in shop windows. Lee Friedlander mapped his own shadow and reflection across the American landscape. What draws them—and what should draw you—is that reflections multiply the visual information in a frame without cluttering it. They add ambiguity, depth, and a quiet poetry that rewards slow looking.

A single plane shows one world. Reflections layer multiple planes of reality—puddles, glass, mirrored surfaces—creating images that reward prolonged attention.

Key Insight

Reflections do not simply double an image—they introduce ambiguity, depth, and visual tension. The viewer's eye moves between planes, uncertain of what is real and what is mirrored. This productive uncertainty is what makes reflected images so compelling.

Reflection vs. Transparency

Glass does two things simultaneously: it reflects the world in front of it and transmits the world behind it. Every window is both a mirror and a portal. The balance between these two properties depends on the relative brightness of each side. A brightly lit interior behind dark glass will show through clearly; a dark interior behind sunlit glass will turn the pane into a near-perfect mirror. Understanding this physics is the foundation of working with glass.

The balance between reflection and transparency depends on relative brightness. Bright exteriors create mirror-like glass; bright interiors let you see through. The most compelling images often occur in the delicate middle ground where both are visible.

The Photographer's Lever

You control the balance between reflection and transparency by choosing your angle and your exposure. Shoot at a steeper angle to the glass and reflections intensify. Expose for the brighter side and the darker plane fades; expose for the darker side and the brighter plane washes out. This is not a limitation—it is your creative instrument.

Windows as Two Worlds

A window is a threshold. It separates inside from outside, private from public, warmth from cold, stillness from motion. When you photograph through or into a window, you capture both worlds at once. The interior subject—a person reading, a barista at work, a couple at dinner—exists simultaneously with the reflected street: passing cars, pedestrians, sky. This juxtaposition is the visual equivalent of a literary double meaning.

A café window merges two realities: the warm interior seen through the glass and the ghostly street reflected on its surface. The window frame becomes a compositional grid dividing these layered worlds.

The most evocative window photographs find a visual rhyme between the two worlds. A solitary figure inside echoed by a solitary figure passing outside. A warm interior contrasting with a cold, grey street. The window does not merely separate these realities—it weaves them into a single image that neither side could produce alone.

  • Shoot at an angle to increase the reflected layer's intensity while still seeing through
  • Use window mullions as compositional dividers—they create natural grids
  • Wait for the interior subject to be still while the reflected world moves, creating contrast between stillness and motion
  • Evening is ideal—interior lights come on while the street still has ambient light, balancing both planes

Puddles, Mirrors, Storefronts, Car Glass

Each reflective surface has its own visual character. Learning to distinguish them—and to seek out each deliberately—gives you a far wider vocabulary of reflection.

Four surfaces, four visual languages. Puddles invert and ripple. Storefronts layer interior and exterior. Car glass curves and distorts. Architectural mirrors fragment the world into geometric repetition.

  • Puddles — get low. The lower your angle, the more of the sky and buildings you capture in the water's surface
  • Storefronts — the most versatile surface. You can control both planes by adjusting angle, distance, and exposure
  • Car windows — curved glass creates distortion and motion blur. Parked cars offer more control; moving cars offer surprise
  • Mirrored architecture — modern glass buildings become canvases for the surrounding city, breaking it into modular fragments

Reversal & Disorientation

Reflections reverse the world. Left becomes right. Up becomes down in a puddle. This reversal is not merely geometric—it is psychological. A reflected image feels uncanny, slightly dreamlike. The viewer senses that something is transposed, even before consciously recognizing the reflection. This productive disorientation is one of your most powerful tools.

Consider rotating a puddle reflection 180 degrees. Suddenly the reflected city looks almost real—but not quite. The light falls wrong. The perspective tilts subtly. This near-reality is deeply unsettling and visually magnetic. Some of the most celebrated reflection photographs play exactly this game: they present the viewer with an image that demands a second look, a moment of recalibration.

Rotating a puddle photograph 180° produces a disorienting near-reality. The reflected world almost passes for the real one—but something feels uncanny. This visual puzzle draws the viewer in.

The Uncanny Double

Reflections carry a psychological charge that goes beyond optics. A reflected figure is a ghostly double—present but intangible, recognizable but reversed. When you place a person alongside their reflection, you create a visual tension between the real and the spectral. This is why reflection photographs often feel poetic: they touch on identity, impermanence, and the thin membrane between worlds.

Layering Through Glass

The most sophisticated reflection photographs do not simply show a reflection—they combine multiple visual planes into a single, coherent image. Through a pane of glass, you might simultaneously see: the interior scene, the reflected exterior, your own silhouette, and perhaps a second reflection from a surface deeper inside. Each layer adds visual complexity and narrative possibility.

A layered glass photograph collapses three spatial planes into one image: the deep interior seen through the glass, reflections on the glass surface, and the foreground including the photographer's own silhouette.

The art lies in balancing these layers so that each remains legible without overwhelming the others. Too much reflection obscures the interior. Too little and you lose the layered quality. The best glass photographs achieve a transparency of meaning: you read them as a single image, but as you look longer, individual layers emerge and recede, creating a visual rhythm that echoes the experience of standing before the glass itself.

  • Choose scenes where layers complement rather than compete—a still interior with a moving reflected street
  • Use your own reflection deliberately, as Friedlander did—the photographer becomes part of the scene
  • Seek out double glass (bus shelters, phone booths) for even more complex layering
  • Post-processing can adjust layer visibility through selective contrast and exposure, but the foundation must be captured in-camera

Exposure Considerations

Reflected scenes almost always present an exposure challenge. The reflected plane and the seen-through plane rarely share the same brightness. A sunlit reflection on glass might be several stops brighter than the dim interior behind it. Your exposure choice determines which layer dominates—and this decision is creative, not merely technical.

Three exposure strategies for the same window scene. Exposing for the reflection silhouettes the interior; exposing for the interior washes out the reflection. The balanced middle ground preserves the layered ambiguity that makes reflection photographs compelling.

Practical Approach

Use spot metering on the brighter layer, then open up 1–1.5 stops. This typically brings both planes into a readable range. If shooting digital, expose to the right (slightly bright) and recover in post—you can darken a reflection more easily than you can pull detail from a lost shadow. Bracketing three exposures at ±1 EV is insurance for complex scenes.

Timing with Reflections

Reflections transform composition into a waiting game. You have found the glass, chosen your angle, set your exposure. Now you need the right figure to enter the reflected frame—or both frames simultaneously. This demands the patience and anticipation that defines great street photography, multiplied by the complexity of managing two visual planes at once.

The reflected figure may enter from a direction the viewer doesn't expect. A person walking behind you appears in the glass moving toward the interior subject. A cyclist reflected in a puddle seems to ride upside down. These surprises cannot be manufactured—they can only be anticipated and waited for.

Four moments at the same window. Only in the third does the reflected figure complement the interior subject without overlapping. This alignment is fleeting—preparation and patience are everything.

Common Mistakes

Reflection photography invites a particular set of errors. The medium is seductive—any glass surface seems to promise a layered masterpiece—but without discipline, the results are muddy, confusing, or simply dull. Recognizing these pitfalls will save you from the most common traps.

✗ Visual Clutter

✓ Clean Separation

  • Too many layers — when reflection, interior, signage, and your own shadow all compete, the image becomes noise. Simplify ruthlessly
  • Weak reflections — a faint, barely-there reflection adds nothing. Either commit to a strong reflected image or abandon the reflection entirely
  • Unreadable ambiguity — some confusion is poetic; total confusion is frustrating. The viewer must be able to eventually decode the image
  • Dirty glass — smudges, fingerprints, and grime flatten the image. Look for clean surfaces, or use the texture deliberately
  • Centering the reflection — placing the reflected subject dead center rarely works. Apply the same compositional rules you would to any subject
  • Ignoring the frame edge — reflected elements cut by the frame edge can look accidental. Be deliberate about what enters and exits

Practice Exercises

Assignment: The Puddle Study

After a rain, spend one hour photographing only puddles. Get as low as possible—kneel, crouch, or set your camera on the ground. Capture at least ten images where the reflected world is the dominant subject. Experiment with rotating your favorites 180° in post-processing. Which images create productive disorientation? Which simply look wrong?

Assignment: Window Portraits

Find a café, restaurant, or shop with a large street-facing window. Position yourself outside and compose a scene that includes both the interior subject and a reflected element. Wait for at least three different reflected figures to pass. Review the results: in which frames do the two worlds create a visual dialogue? What makes the interaction work?

Assignment: Exposure Bracketing

Choose a window scene where the interior and reflected exterior differ in brightness. Shoot three versions: one exposing for the reflection, one for the interior, and one balanced between the two. Compare the results side by side. Notice how exposure choice completely changes the story the image tells—the same glass, three different photographs.

Assignment: Self-Portrait in Glass

In the tradition of Lee Friedlander, spend an afternoon incorporating your own reflection into your photographs. Use shop windows, car mirrors, polished metal surfaces. Your presence should feel intentional, not accidental. Explore how your silhouette interacts with the scenes behind and within the glass. Make at least fifteen images where your reflection is a compositional element, not an intruder.

Assignment: One Surface, Ten Images

Choose a single reflective surface—one particular window, one puddle, one mirrored wall—and make ten distinctly different images from it over the course of an hour. Change your angle, your distance, your exposure, your timing. Discover how many variations a single surface can yield when you exhaust the obvious and push into the unexpected.
