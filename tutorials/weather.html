Tutorials / Weather & Atmosphere

Embracing the Elements

Weather transforms the ordinary into the extraordinary. Learn to work with rain, fog, snow, and light to create atmospheric images that evoke emotion and mood.

Rain Photography

Rain creates one of the most photogenic conditions for street photography. Wet surfaces become mirrors, umbrellas add graphic elements, and the mood shifts dramatically. The key is learning to see these opportunities.

Rain creates reflections, silhouettes, and graphic elements like umbrellas.

Working with Reflections

Wet pavement transforms streets into mirrors. Get low to exaggerate reflections—often the reflection is more interesting than the subject itself. Look for neon signs, streetlights, and colorful storefronts reflected in puddles.

  • Shoot immediately after rain stops for maximum reflection without obstructing drops
  • Umbrellas create natural frames and add splashes of color
  • Backlit rain creates visible streaks—expose for highlights
  • People move differently in rain—faster, more purposeful, more expressive

Fog & Mist

Fog is the street photographer's gift. It simplifies backgrounds, creates natural layers, and adds an ethereal quality that transforms familiar locations into mysterious scenes. The reduced visibility forces focus on what's essential.

Fog creates natural depth layers—objects fade progressively into the mist.

  • Arrive early—fog often burns off by mid-morning
  • Use silhouettes against the bright fog background
  • Overexpose slightly to maintain the ethereal quality
  • Single subjects isolated in fog create powerful minimalist images
  • Light sources create beautiful halos and glow effects

Snow & Winter

Snow transforms cities into high-key wonderlands. The challenge lies in exposure—cameras want to make snow gray. The reward is simplified scenes, muted colors, and people behaving differently in the cold.

Snow scenes require +1 to +2 stops exposure compensation to render white as white.

Exposure Compensation

Your camera's meter sees snow and tries to make it 18% gray. Override this by adding +1 to +2 stops of exposure compensation. Check your histogram—the snow should push toward the right edge without clipping.

  • Falling snow shows best against dark backgrounds
  • Fresh snow creates clean, simplified compositions
  • People bundled up create anonymous, universal figures
  • Color accents (red scarves, umbrellas) pop against white
  • Protect your camera from moisture when moving between warm and cold

Harsh Sunlight

Conventional wisdom says avoid midday sun. But street photographers have learned to embrace it. The high contrast, sharp shadows, and dramatic light can create images impossible at any other time.

Harsh midday light creates dramatic boundaries between light and shadow.

  • Position yourself where subjects walk from shadow into light
  • Expose for highlights—let shadows go completely black
  • Look for architectural shadows creating geometric patterns
  • The transition zone between light and shadow is most dramatic
  • Embrace the high contrast—it's the point, not a problem

Golden Hour Magic

The hour after sunrise and before sunset transforms everything it touches. The low sun angle creates long shadows, warm tones wrap subjects in flattering light, and ordinary scenes become cinematic.

Low sun creates long shadows, rim lighting on subjects, and warm color throughout.

Why Golden Hour Works

The sun's low angle means light travels through more atmosphere, filtering out blue wavelengths and leaving warm tones. Shadows stretch dramatically, adding depth. The soft quality wraps around subjects, creating dimension without harsh contrast.

  • Plan your location in advance—golden hour is brief
  • Shoot into the sun for silhouettes and rim lighting
  • Long shadows become compositional elements themselves
  • The warm light flatters skin tones for portraits
  • Windows and glass become golden mirrors

Blue Hour & Twilight

The transition between day and night offers a brief window of magic. Artificial lights begin to glow while natural light remains. This balance of warm and cool creates an otherworldly atmosphere.

Blue hour balances cool natural light with warm artificial sources—a photographer's dream.

  • Blue hour lasts only 20-40 minutes—scout locations beforehand
  • Balance artificial and natural light for color contrast
  • Tripod or higher ISO needed as light fades quickly
  • The deep blue sky provides beautiful negative space
  • Reflections on wet streets amplify the color interplay

Night & Artificial Light

Night photography reveals a different city. Neon signs, streetlamps, car headlights—each creates its own atmosphere. The challenge shifts from too much light to finding enough of it.

Night offers multiple light sources: neon, streetlamps, headlights—each with its own color and quality.

Finding Light in Darkness

Night photography isn't about shooting in darkness—it's about shooting the light that exists in darkness. Position yourself where artificial light illuminates your subjects: under streetlamps, near shop windows, in the glow of neon signs.

  • High ISO with fast lenses—accept some grain
  • Pool of light method: wait for subjects to walk through lit areas
  • Mixed color temperatures create visual interest
  • Wet streets reflect and multiply available light
  • Long exposures create light trails from moving vehicles

Protecting Your Gear

Weather creates opportunities, but it also threatens equipment. With proper preparation, you can shoot confidently in almost any conditions while keeping your camera safe.

Each weather condition requires specific protective measures for your equipment.

Simple Solutions

Avoid This

  • Keep a small towel in your bag for wiping equipment
  • Ziplock bags protect during sudden weather changes
  • Let camera temperature equalize before removing from bag
  • Insurance covers what preparation cannot prevent
  • Some weather damage creates happy accidents—don't fear every drop

Practice Exercises

Assignment: Rain Day

Go out during or immediately after a rainstorm. Focus exclusively on reflections—find at least three different types: puddle reflections, wet pavement reflections, and window reflections. Notice how rain changes human behavior and capture those moments.

Assignment: Weather Mood Board

Over one month, photograph the same location in at least four different weather conditions: sunny, overcast, rainy, and foggy (or snowy if available). Compare how weather transforms the mood and story of that single place.

Assignment: Light Transition

Choose an evening and photograph continuously from golden hour through blue hour into night. Document how the changing light alters your subjects, the color palette, and the overall atmosphere. Note which moments feel most compelling.
